Where the app actually stands today

What is inside Lifestead, and how it is put together

Lifestead is an everything-about-a-person's-life app, tagline "Everything in your life has a place." It started with, and runs deepest on, food and autoimmune health: 19 tracked conditions, each a target for the same depth, alone or combined. It is also for somebody who needs an outside place to hold the details of daily living so they stop taking up room in their head, through Life, Schedules, reminders and capture. Garden, budget, family, interests, learning and the body are all in scope.

Its job in food and health is finding the personal patterns between what someone eats and how their body responds, and doing that work for the person. Records stay on the person's devices, and no company server holds them.

Kept up to date as the app changes. Last refreshed 2026-11-12 (1.0.87.4). The reading is called the Library: the purple group of flip cards on Home and the reading lenses on Life and Garden. To click through the navigation instead of reading about it, see the companion Lifestead Walkthrough.

How the app is navigated

There's no bottom tab bar. The whole app runs on a hub-and-spoke model built specifically for this project.

TabHub

A central popup menu (reached from a floating button) replaces a persistent bottom tab bar. It shows every top-level tab at once, color-coded by its own jewel tone sampled from the app's commissioned butterfly artwork, plus left/right swipe gestures for moving between tabs directly.

LensHub

Most tabs (Life, Food, Garden, Interests, Schedules, Signals, Insights, Trends, Reports) have their own internal "lenses": sub-views reached through a second, page-local popup menu rather than a wall of sub-tabs. Picking a tab from TabHub opens the tab itself, and the box in the lower corner names the button to tap for its lenses.

The edge tabs

Small tabs sit at the bottom edges of every screen. On the thumb side, from the bottom: quick access (Ask Your Records, Where Is It, Store Its Location, notes, Plan My Meals, Simple View and Low Stimulation), Voice Control, a tab wearing a flip card (one card under a curved two-way arrow), and search on screens that have a search box. On the far side: the navigation switch, which moves the menus to the other hand. The flip card tab opens Flash Cards (Language Basics, Languages, Learn) and Reading Material (Conditions, Earth Matters, Health Literacy, Horticulture, Search Reading, and the Library while Home shows it). Opening any edge tab while another menu is open switches straight to it.

The ten tabs, in menu and swipe order: Home, Life, Food, Garden, Interests, Schedules, Signals, Insights, Trends, Reports, then Profile and Info in the same menu. One list (constants/tabs.ts) drives the menu, the swipe order and the header dots together. The reading lives on Life (Conditions, Health Literacy, Earth Matters, Search Reading), Garden (Horticulture) and Food (recipes). Which hand works the navigation is a live setting on each device, and the hubs mirror to match it. A lens is a subject holding several bands behind the fold, and a thing is defined where it lives and gets its timeline on Schedules.

Home has its own corner menu. It is deliberately not a scroll-to: every section on Home is a window onto a function living in another tab, so picking one goes to that function, exactly as tapping the card does. The single exception is the Library (the flip cards, in a purple group of their own under a library icon), whose cards are the content rather than a shortcut, so it scrolls to itself. Each icon wears the colour of the tab it leads to, so the grouping and the colouring compound and the grid reads as blocks.

The menus never open themselves: the box in the lower corner is the one place that names the button to tap. The active ring and menu border follow the chosen Ground Theme rather than a fixed teal, lightened by a measured amount first: taken raw, the theme's own button colour measures between 2.26:1 and 3.20:1 against the menu surface, under the 3:1 floor on four of the five themes.

Every screen takes one look: full-width bands with a 4px accent bar in the tab's colour and hairlines above and below, no right edge, each one folding to a single row carrying its name and count until tapped, with what is left open remembered across launches. Rows inside a band are inset boxes at one standard gap, forms stay open as plain band boxes, and a menu row is an action band. The box in the lower corner shows the same icon beside "Tap the {tab} button in the corner to pick a tool", the tab name in that tab's colour, at a fixed size on every tab.

Every tab's resting screen is empty on purpose: the background and nothing else. A tab's background choice reaches it: a tab set to Off, Generic or a photo somebody added shows that from the moment the tab opens, while the default leaves the resting screen to the single shared scene. These screens are meant to become where a person's use of the app shows over time, and nothing can grow on a screen already full of links to somewhere else. That growth is designed and not built.

Why this shape: a persistent tab bar cannot hold ten tabs, and a picker that pops open on every arrival gets in the way. The hub-and-spoke pattern keeps every tab one tap away and leaves the screen to the tab.

Home

A personalized daily dashboard: the app's entry point, not a generic landing screen, and the one screen someone actually gets to shape into their own.

What's on it

Built

  • Today's Fuel Gauges: nutrient rings against DRI targets
  • Today's Check-In: an one-question daily mood/symptom picker, pulling from a 30-tag vocabulary across 7 categories
  • Symptom check-in reminder: a banner that surfaces automatically every 30 days since the last periodic assessment
  • Quick actions: fast blood-pressure/exercise logging, shortcuts into other tabs

  • Grocery List: turn the next few days of scheduled meals into a list you can shop from
  • Days Until: two cards: the counters in the garden, in the Garden group, and the ones about anything else, in the Life group. Each shows up to five that are still running, starts a new one without leaving Home, and marks one done. Both are individually toggleable from Profile like every other Home section
  • Library: one flip card per reading category, each in the colour and icon of the tab its entry lives on (Life for a condition, Health Literacy or Earth Matters; Garden for Horticulture; Food for a recipe), with a Read More pill opening the entry in place on that tab, brought to the top of the screen. The cards run alphabetically by category, which also keeps each tab's cards together. The cards keep a group of their own at the bottom of Home, under Life, in purple, named Library under a library icon

Home carries the app's answer to its most-named risk: whether anyone logs consistently enough, for long enough, for correlation to mean anything. The principle is to separate capture from classification, so capture takes two seconds and working out what it was can happen afterwards, on the phone. Four ways in, none of which needs an off-device model. Find a Meal searches every meal already logged and every meal favourite in SQL rather than over a loaded page, so a name past the load cap is still reachable by typing it, and offers four things that actually happen: log it now, log it earlier today, schedule it, or use it instead of a meal that was planned. Say What You Ate is pointed at the two cases nothing else covers, a restaurant plate with no barcode and no recipe anywhere, and a meal eaten instead of a planned one that would otherwise sit unresolved forever. Photograph it now stores a draft rather than an empty meal, deliberately, since a meal row with no ingredients would count as logged everywhere in the app while contributing nothing. And a barcode scan offers Log This as Eaten as well as answering "should I buy this".

Speech is recognised on the phone: the app asks for on-device recognition and checks the locale is installed before requesting it, falls back to the phone's network recogniser otherwise, and the review card says which mode it got.

The app introduces its own main button. The control that reaches all ten tabs carries no circle, no fill and no label at rest, deliberately, and to someone who has never opened the app it is a round button at the bottom of the screen, wearing the Lifestead mark, with every tool behind it. A welcome shown once dims everything except that button, and a pointer stays above it until it has actually been tapped, since reading about a button is not the same as knowing where it is. Both can be replayed from Profile.

Every one of Home's content sections (the welcome greeting stays fixed, always available) can be turned off from Profile's "Home Screen" card, and every one can be moved up or down independently of the others. Turning every section off shows a plain explanatory message instead of a blank screen.

The welcome card itself collapses into a small floating badge in the top-left corner 30 seconds after the app opens (or immediately on swiping to another tab), staying out of the way of the content beneath it, and expands back to full size on tap before auto-collapsing again.

The header carries one dot per non-Home tab under its title, showing at a glance which tabs have activity, with a band underneath reserved for what grows from a person's records. Tapping the header's "{name}'s Lifestead" title opens a Timeline screen, a placeholder for the full life-history view that is planned.

Your Guide

The guided way through the app. Its sections are named for what they hold.

How it works

Built

The heading reads Start Here at first, then Getting Set Up, then All Set, More When You Want It. It starts with six sections: What Matters to You, Keeping Your Records Safe, What You Take, What Your Days Hold, What You Eat, Drink and Feel, and What Your Records Show. What Matters to You asks "What parts of your life do you want Lifestead to follow?" and each part chosen (Health, Food, Movement, Home, Garden, Money, Routines, Work, Family, Interests) can add a section after those six. Choosing never hides anything anywhere in the app.

An item is done when its record exists. It carries the date that record went in, and if the record goes it reopens and says so. There is no "step 3 of 8", no percentage, no praise and no blame, and every item links to the exact tab and lens where it is done.

It lives in five places: a card on Home that can be folded and moved but not turned off, since it is how somebody finds their way back to a step that dropped off; a full page; an entry in Profile; a "Where this fits in Your Guide" line at the foot of every help sheet; and the empty states of Pattern Finder, Trends and Reports, which say which step would give them something to show.

Guides for every part of life

Each part of life has a guide, step by step, and the steps are grouped by when they happen (Start here, Every day, Later on, When it happens) and ordered toward the first thing the part can show back. Each step can be read Short or Step by step, a choice that is remembered.

Back to Your Guide

Any screen opened from a Your Guide line carries one pill in the top right that goes back to the same place in the guide, so following a step never means finding the way back by hand.

Walk me through it

A step can be walked. A strip at the top of the screen shows one line at a time (tap the round button, pick Life, open My Meds) and moves on by itself as each screen is reached. It has Next, Back, Your Guide and Stop, folds to a single line when it is in the way, and watches for the record: once it exists, the strip says it is saved and what it leads to.

The button it names is outlined

On the Start here and Every day steps, the button a walk line names is drawn with an outline on the screen itself: the round button, the corner button and My Items get a round one, anything else a box. An outline takes up no room, so marking a button never moves the screen around it, and nothing is outlined once the record is saved or the strip is closed.

By tab

The guide also reads by tab: where records go in (Life, Schedules, Signals, Food, Garden) and what records give back (Insights, Trends, Reports), each tab saying what it gives and naming anything from another tab that has to exist first.

An interview, then a tour

It opens as an interview, one question at a time: conditions, neurodivergence, parts of life, where to start, meds and supplements, the stage of each staged condition, and eating style. A tour follows, describing each whole tab: what it does for you, what you can count on it for, every lens in one line, and the fastest way in, the first step not yet done marked Start with this.

The output lenses

Trends, Insights and Reports each read records kept on other tabs, and every one of their lenses is listed in the tour.

Life

Everything about running a life that is not food or a symptom, and the one place the app can join those to the rest.

Eighteen lenses

Built
Grocery List Conditions Health Literacy Earth Matters Search Reading Finances Work Upkeep Emergency My Meds Kitchen Movement Routines To Do List Days Until

Conditions renders every condition's reading in the System Recipes layout: one fold band per condition with its full-colour icon, topic folds and subgroups inside, entries opening in place, one search box over the page. Three lists never mix: My Conditions (Profile's selections), Family (a local roster, whose members' conditions join the Meal Plan's scope when their include-in-meal-plan switch is on), and Other Conditions (the "curious about" list, readable here and never feeding scoring, planning or advisories).

Health Literacy, Earth Matters and Search Reading sit beside it. Health Literacy is the condition-agnostic layer, with the Glossary as a band inside it; Earth Matters is the food-system and planet reading; Search Reading searches every entry in the library, with hits expanding in place and a hit that lives on Garden or Food going there and arriving open. All three render on the same fold bands as Conditions.

Finances is the largest area, and it rests on one decision: it reads what already exists rather than asking anyone to retype it. Priced grocery trips and hands-on therapy costs are read where they live and copied nowhere, because copying is exactly how a corrected price ends up disagreeing with a stale duplicate. It covers accounts and net worth, per-category budget limits, debt payoff simulated month by month with a rolling commitment, sinking funds, goals, and income streams. Two things make it worth having inside a health app rather than replaceable by Monarch: a health-money layer (deductible and out-of-pocket standing, HSA versus FSA, an Explanation of Benefits check whose whole value is what it leaves out, and cost per condition) and a refusal to invent figures.

That refusal is the throughline. A goal costs money, or time, or goods, and those are never blended into one percentage, because $500 and 20 hours with half of each done is two facts rather than "50% done". Hours are never converted to dollars at an assumed rate. A market-rate account is measured rather than projected, and money paid in is asked about separately, since a balance rising from 40,000 to 46,000 could be a 30% return or a 5,000 deposit. A trade of surplus produce is valued only when a recorded price exists from that person's own past shopping, and then only as an avoided cost, never as income.

Work covers both readings of one question: benefits going unclaimed, and the value in the work itself. It names no country, statute or scheme anywhere, and a test enforces that, because almost everything here is jurisdiction-specific enough that a confident claim would be wrong for most readers. Autonomy, competence and relatedness are attributed to Self-Determination Theory rather than presented as the app's idea.

Upkeep deliberately does not reuse the bill scheduling beside it, and the distinction is exact: a bill is calendar-anchored, so rent arrives on the 1st whether or not you did anything, while a service is last-done-anchored, so a boiler serviced in March is next due the following March rather than next January.

Emergency & Essentials leads with a warning rather than a feature, and it is not dismissible: an app on a locked phone is not reachable by a paramedic, and anyone who comes away believing this replaces a bracelet or the medical ID their phone already has is worse off. The card carries drug allergies as well as food allergies, since on an emergency card a drug allergy is arguably the highest-stakes line there is. An absent field is absent rather than printed blank, since "Drug allergies: none recorded" reads as "no allergies" to someone scanning it.

Kitchen is what is actually in the house, fed by three sources: garden and fermentation harvests, which carry a measured remaining amount, and everything added by hand or put there by ticking a grocery line off. The line it draws is the same honesty rule the grocery list turns on. A harvest can be subtracted because it is drawn down as it gets used; a past purchase cannot, because nothing decrements it as it gets eaten, so it surfaces as "you bought this 2 days ago, worth checking" and never as an amount. Household consumables live in their own inventory beside the food one, since almost everything the app does with a food is meaningless for bleach. Making a dish moves stock both ways, and it is never silent: the plan is shown, then confirmed, because saving a recipe is not cooking it.

My Meds is the one place a prescription, an over-the-counter drug or a supplement is added, edited, paused or removed: pick a prescription or OTC item from a cited reference list where one exists or enter it yourself, give a supplement its nutrients and, where the app has researched them, the specific form (magnesium glycinate against oxide, since the form changes how well it absorbs), and see when to take it, what to avoid pairing it with, and how much of that nutrient food already covered today. It sets no times itself: each med has a Schedule it button that opens Schedules > Meds with that med ready, which is the whole reason it sits on Life beside Kitchen rather than on Schedules. What you take is a thing you own; when you take it is a schedule.

Days Until counts down to anything. A counter can be about a passport in the post, a course starting, a cast coming off or a warranty running out, and this lens is every counter of either kind on one list, ordered by when it lands, a garden one naming its area and a free one showing whatever was written under it. Starting one here picks a garden area or Anything. It is a second table rather than the garden one with the area left blank, deliberately: a garden counter travels to Past Areas with its area and counts toward whether that area still holds records, and neither is true of a free one. One piece of arithmetic serves both, so both count whole calendar days, both read Today on the day, both keep counting as "N days over" rather than disappearing, and both raise the same reminder that morning, differing only in which tab a tap on it lands on. Garden > Days Until stays about the garden.

Food

Twelve focused builders for constructing a meal from raw ingredients, deliberately not a generic "log a branded product" tracker. The app's own stated direction favors home cooking over commercial/pre-made items; a dedicated "Scan a Product" barcode flow handles a store-bought or packaged item separately, rather than folding it into these 12 whole-food builders.

The twelve builders

Built
Meal Sides Salads & Bowls Smoothies Fermentation Beverages Snacks Baked Goods Soups Sauces Handhelds Desserts

Meal Builder is the assembler: it doesn't add raw ingredients itself, it pulls together saved output from the other eleven (pick how much of a saved Side or Salad was actually eaten, log it as one meal). It can log something right now, favorite it, or schedule it for later the same day.

Every one of the other eleven follows the same shape: name it, add ingredients from a category-scoped food search, get a live nutrient/6-Dimensions preview, then save it as a standalone record and/or a reusable favorite. Several carry their own specific checks: a raw-goitrogenic-load warning when two or more raw cruciferous ingredients are combined (Salads & Bowls, Smoothies), and non-blocking advisory rows for alcohol, coffee, and juice content where relevant.

Beverages, Fermentation, Soups, Sauces, and Desserts (the five builders whose category allowlist includes Alcohol) also carry an inline ABV, residual-sugar and pours calorie calculator: a chemistry estimate (grams of ethanol from volume × pours × ABV% × 0.789, calories at 7 kcal/g, plus a cited alcohol-retention-by-cooking-method table for anything simmered or reduced) for a bottle that matches no single database row closely enough, such as an off-catalog proof, a homemade wine, more than one pour or a reduced sauce. Filling in Volume and ABV makes the calculator's result the tracked value for that ingredient, filling in Quantity and Unit and overriding the database row for calories and carbs (every other nutrient stays at zero, matching how thoroughly distillation strips out non-ethanol content), and a note above Quantity and Unit says so while it is active. Beer is left out, since dextrins and starches survive brewing as unfermented carbs a formula like this would undercount.

Tapping Beverages fresh (not editing or reusing a saved one) opens a subcategory picker first: Cocktails & Mixology, Infusions & Brews, Juices & Nectars, Hydration & Wellness, and Performance & Protein each narrow the ingredient search to match, while Smoothies and Fermentation open those two builders.

Every direct-ingredient builder opens with Find a Recipe at the top (My Kitchen, Recipes Shared With Me, the built-in Recipes, My Favorites), closed by Or Create a New Recipe above the blank form. The built-in recipes are assembled from ingredients checked against the reference database, with Omnivore, Vegan and Vegetarian versions, and each is reachable from a "Build This Recipe" button on the Recipes lens. Fermentation Builder's recipes carry the specific bacterial strains used, chosen from a strain picker drawn from the cited reading. Every ingredient in the recipes is one a person can buy at an ordinary supermarket, and where a fruit is seasonal or sold mostly dried, the recipe says what to use instead.

Every builder computes the same depth the curated recipes carry, live, for anything a person builds: severity-tiered condition cautions, diet tags and healing-stage notes, from the app's scoring functions (lib/recipeDepth.ts). A Preview Full Report step, in both create and edit mode, leads with a bar chart of nutrient content against the person's daily target, then gives each tracked condition a plain Clean, Caution or Not Recommended verdict beside a chart of that condition's dimensions: a radar for Hashimoto's six (D1 to D6) and any condition with three or more dimensions, a coloured row per dimension for the others. For any tracked condition with a staging model, the report names the person's declared stage, or offers a Set My Stage picker in place that never leaves the builder. A "Worth Knowing for Your Healing Stage" card shows once a stage is declared, with each matching note listed once, and says plainly when nothing is flagged. Then "Save As Is" or "Go Back and Adjust." Meal Builder works the depth out fresh each time from the ingredients of every component it holds, ahead of "Log This Now" and "Save Changes."

Everything reached from the Food tab renders inside the Food tab: Log or Schedule a Meal, the My Kitchen lists, a saved dish's detail, a scanned product and the scanner itself are all Food lenses, on Food's background with the footer and both hub buttons in place. Log or Schedule a Meal lists every system recipe with its description and lets a dish be ticked out of any meal, with ticks adding up across meals into one combination shown whole before it is built, and an opened meal scrolls itself to the top.

The My Foods menu leads with Log or Schedule a Meal and tracks Saved and Favorite counts per builder, plus Favorite Meals, all reachable and editable from one place. My Kitchen and My Favorites give the same items a deeper browsing surface, with computed nutrition and condition detail, scheduling and sharing. The menu also carries Scan a Product and My Food Products, a screen for browsing, editing and tracking the price history of anything scanned in at the store. Every tab with both a lens picker and a hub like My Foods, My Garden or My Reports shows that hub as a tile at the top left of the picker as well, and tapping it closes the picker first. Tapping Food opens on the tab's background with both hub buttons in place.

Garden

An Agricultural Zone Finder plus home-gardening tracking, built specifically so growing your own food ties directly into the rest of the app rather than staying separate reading material.

The eleven lenses

Built
  • My Zone: a USDA Plant Hardiness Zone picker (1a–13b), cross-referencing the Horticulture lens's climate-band crop guidance for whichever zone is set
  • Plots & Plantings: structured garden areas (location type, space type, sunlight exposure, dimensions, and a per-area zone that defaults from Profile but stays independently editable) and what's actually planted in each, tied to a reference food, not a free-text crop name
  • Days Until: every Days Until counter in the garden on one band, soonest first, and a place to start one by picking its area
  • Harvest Log: logging what was actually picked, from something already tracked as a planting (not the whole food reference database), with a remaining-inventory list and a quick way to add a garden task to the Schedule
  • Upcoming Tasks: every garden task scheduled
  • Compost: one band per pile, bin, tumbler, worm bin or trench: what went in, turns and waterings, checks, and what it feeds
  • Growing Costs: money spent to grow food, kept apart per area or cost group, with the Electricity band comparing bills before and since the grow
  • Horticulture: the cited home-gardening reading (the zone framework, containers, beginner crops, season extension, soil, indoor growing, and why growing things belongs in a health app at all), named so it says it is about learning

The zone is set once (in either the Garden tab or Profile; both write to the same value) and stays synced between the two.

The payoff: a harvest with unused inventory shows up as a selectable "From Your Harvest" ingredient the next time any of the 11 Food builders' own ingredient picker opens, resolved into the exact same reference-database food identity as anything else, so nutrition and 6-DFF scoring for something you grew yourself is never a second, separate concept. A harvest itself is deliberately scoped to something already growing: "Add a Harvest" picks from your own tracked plantings (still growing or already producing more than once, like tomatoes or squash) rather than opening the whole reference database: a planting still comes from any food, but a harvest only comes from a planting. Logging a garden task ("water the tomatoes") creates a repeatable entry in the same underlying Schedule data every other scheduled item uses.

Areas. A garden area is in-ground, a raised bed, containers and pots, a tent, or a space the person names. An indoor area asks what lights it (kind of light, make or model, how many, watts each, hours a day, on a timer, spectrum, and the plant stage it suits) in place of how much direct sun; a greenhouse asks the sun question and whether lights were added; outdoors asks the sun question only. Every area has a Grow Setup fold for lights, containers, hydroponics, a humidifier or fogger, fans, exhaust, air filters, climate control, water filtration, timers, meters and any kind the person names, each with a quantity, watts and hours a day, what it cost and when, and an ongoing amount. Each planting carries a status (Growing, Harvested, Failed, Pulled out). An area still growing something cannot be moved to Past Areas, and an area with anything recorded under it is never deleted: it moves to a Past Areas band below the current ones, where what grew there stays readable, with Bring it back.

Days Until counters. Under an area, Add a Days Until Counter takes a name (germination, transplanting out, the first harvest), how many days, the day it started, and, if wanted, one planting in the area. Growing Costs. Each cost is a finance entry in the Garden & growing supplies category, so the household budget on Life > Finances sees the same money once. The top card is the net for the whole garden, never one crop: kept harvests and produce given to the person, each valued only at a price they have recorded paying, less every growing cost, with a By Area band and a rollup by indoors, greenhouse and outdoors. The Electricity band takes each bill's period, amount and kWh, compares every bill after the one marked as from before the grow per day, prices the setup's recorded wattage and hours, and records the monthly difference as a growing cost only when asked.

Compost. Each pile records what went in and whether it was green or brown, turns, waterings, a temperature reading, a squeeze test for moisture, finished compost taken out, and which area or cost group it feeds. It reads back what it needs from what was recorded and nothing else: a greens-to-browns lean, a dry or wet last check, too long since a turn, whether it reached 131°F / 55°C. Compost from kitchen scraps costs nothing and is never entered.

Harvests on Food. Logging a harvest ends by asking whether to keep it on hand; a kept harvest appears on Food > My Whole Foods with the amount left, drawn down as it is cooked with, beside a From Other Gardens band for produce someone gave the person. Its Money Not Spent band values each at a price the person has recorded paying, as an avoided cost, never income and never a guessed market price.

Every list of names in Garden reads alphabetically, with anything the person named merged in by name, and a list that is a scale (sunlight, plant stage, moisture, units) keeps its order. Any list of kinds, spaces or spectra takes an entry of the person's own, and removing one first asks where to move whatever is using it.

Scope limits: the zone is entered by hand, with no ZIP or postal-code lookup. A harvest can be picked as an ingredient in any builder, but the builders do not draw down how much of it a dish used. A garden task is a Schedule entry, and Garden's Upcoming Tasks lens is where garden tasks are browsed and managed.

Schedules

Each lens is one category's own timeline. A thing is defined where it lives, mostly on Life, and gets its schedule here. Schedules never defines anything.

The ten lenses

Built
Meals Today’s Meals Past Meals Meal Plan Hydration Meds Appointments Upkeep Exercise

Schedules is about the schedule for each category or topic. Prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs and supplements share one Meds timeline, and Upkeep is read straight from Life. The meds themselves are defined in Life > My Meds, a hands-on therapy session is logged on Signals since it has already happened, and the grocery list is built on Life's Grocery List.

Meds is one dose timeline for everything a person takes. Every med being tracked shows its reminder times, today's doses sit at the top to be marked taken or skipped, and the interaction checks that depend on those times sit beside them. Nothing is defined here: "Add or change a med in My Meds" at the top and "Edit in My Meds" on every row go to Life, and each med on Life carries a Schedule it button that lands back here with that med's reminder form open and its group unfolded. Upkeep is the timeline of everything under Life > Upkeep that has a date: overdue, due in the next 45 days, and later, plus anything still missing the piece it needs to be placed on a calendar, listed with what is missing rather than left off so the list looks complete. Done today records a recurring item as done and works out its next date from its own interval, the same as doing it from Life; adding, editing or renewing stays in Life, one tap away from any row, and Life's Upkeep links to the timeline.

Today’s Meals is the one lens meant to be read while cooking rather than while planning or correcting the record. It lists the day in clock order, meals and doses together. Opening a meal shows each component dish’s ingredients and its written steps. It shows the whole day rather than only what has been eaten, since a meal is cooked before it is logged; planned, eaten and skipped are each labelled. A meal typed in directly has no recipe behind it and says so, and a meal built from saved dishes whose steps were never written shows its ingredients and says that plainly, rather than either case rendering an empty panel.

Opening a dose reads the meals around it: which one competes with it, how much of what it carries, how far apart the two are, the cited rule behind it, and a Why? explanation plus the nutrient timing reference on tap. A dose is compared against meals as well as other doses, and fat against the meal rather than the whole day, so levothyroxine at 7:00 beside a yogurt breakfast at 7:30 is caught the same as a calcium capsule at 7:30. The amounts at which an ordinary meal starts to compete are working thresholds rather than doses from a trial, since no source states them, which is why every sentence names the amount found instead of asking to be trusted, and why a meal nothing could be resolved from reads as could not be checked rather than as carrying nothing. Nothing on the lens writes: times, taken, skipped and removed all stay on Meds, so one place holds that record.

Past Meals settles a lapsed scheduled meal into logged data automatically, so Trends and Reports see a meal that was scheduled and never explicitly logged. It carries a portion-correction flow (how much of a past meal was eaten, from 0% up to more than planned), and when that correction touches a food an active trial is riding on, it offers to put the trial back to waiting or shift its start date rather than leaving it wrong.

A shared interaction-rules engine runs underneath Schedules and Insights alike: cited timing-separation and dose-consistency rules (levothyroxine with calcium, iron, magnesium or coffee, and dozens more condition by condition), kept visually separate from the personal rule builder that holds someone's own discovered patterns or a doctor's specific instruction. Rules can be age-based, resolved against the birth date in Profile rather than shown to everyone (an NSAID caution specific to 65+, a pediatric-only DKA complication warning), and can key off a tracked condition directly, not only an active medication.

Meal Plan is the one generator. It builds a single day, or up to six weeks with the same "how many days" picker, live from the person's declared conditions, diet preferences, and a No Carbs, Low Carb or no-restriction choice, pooling only recipes that are safe for every declared condition at once and using the NASEM dietary reference intakes for nutrient-coverage figures. Breakfast draws only from a verified breakfast pool with a live sweetener check (never a smoothie), and the whole day gets the same green, yellow or red rating "Meals You Can Eat" uses. More than one day threads a rotation through the run, every slot preferring whichever recipe has been used fewest times so far, plus two cited weekly frequency rules (fish or seafood at least twice a week, red meat no more than three times a week) enforced day by day. Omnivore, Vegan and Vegetarian recipes all feed it. A generated plan is saved as a record the moment it is made and stays on the screen, through app updates, restarts and syncs, until Generate makes a new one; putting it on the calendar copies its meals into the schedule and leaves the plan in place.

A generated plan follows Profile: the usual meal times set there, and the declared intermittent-fasting eating window. A meal whose usual time falls outside the window is moved inside it (breakfast to the window's open, dinner just inside its close, lunch at the midpoint) rather than scheduled when the person would not be eating. Setting a plan up again skips days already scheduled, so Profile > Meal Plan carries "Apply My Meal Timing Changes to Existing Meals": an in-place time update that keeps the same meals on the same days and leaves anything already logged untouched, rather than a delete-and-rebuild that would orphan the saved dishes each slot owns.

The eating window is never a hard block. Scheduling a meal outside it offers "Pick Another Time" or "Add Meal Anyway", for a missed meal, illness, or any other reason. A meal added that way is saved and marked "Outside eating window" on the schedule, a plain factual label rather than a repeated warning, so the record holds a meal that was eaten instead of omitting it. Rescheduling it back inside the window clears the mark. Trends does not read the mark.

How it's organized

Built

Health Literacy carries a Hands-On & Complementary Therapies topic, and roughly half of its entries report that a therapy did not work, which is what makes them worth the space. A reading order (DIGEST_READING_ORDER in lib/digest/conditionGrouping.ts) puts the shelves that tell a story in sequence: the Green Revolution before the 1972 origins before the certification era, the two soil-depletion studies before the critique before the answer, the zone entries in order, and the soil-microbiome entries from trial to mechanism to synthesis. Everything unlisted reads alphabetically.

Both areas say why they are inside a health app at all: the Old Friends hypothesis (the precise successor to the "hygiene hypothesis": specific environmental organisms are a needed input for the immune system's regulatory development, not "less clean is better"), the Finnish and Russian Karelia comparison (26.6% against 2% birch-pollen sensitization in schoolchildren, and the national health program built on it that cut allergy-related hospital days in half), and, in Horticulture, a 28-day randomized trial showing biodiverse contact shifts a child's immune markers within a month. Both close on the same point, naming the features that carry it (the harvest-to-Food-builder pipeline, the Fermented Foods reading and builder, and the whole-food-first design of every Food builder): a person's body and the land any food comes from were never two separate systems.

Recipes has 411 entries, one per curated recipe across the Food builders, each using that recipe's name, flavor profile and health benefit. Every entry carries a "Build This Recipe" button that opens the matching builder loaded with the ingredients, quantities and prep steps. The 6-Week Whole-Food Meal Plan recipes and their parallel Vegan and Vegetarian tracks are among them (see the Food and Schedules sections for how the plan is set up).

Every recipe carries a computed diet-compatibility tag: Vegan/Vegetarian/Omnivore, plus Mediterranean, Gluten-Free, Dairy-Free, Paleo, AIP, Plant-Based/Flexitarian, and High-Protein wherever the recipe's ingredients support it. Each tag shows as a badge on the recipe's card, and a "Filter by diet" picker on the Recipes lens narrows the whole category (topic menu, shelves and search alike) to what fits. Keto is deliberately left untagged; the app has no reliable per-recipe carb-gram data to back the claim.

Every recipe carries a computed list of which of the 19 tracked conditions it has no significant flagged ingredients for, from the same condition-scoring engine the rest of the app uses. Each tracked condition's page in Life > Conditions leads with a "Meals You Can Eat" topic showing those recipes, filtered by any declared diet preference as well, so someone both Vegan and tracking Hashimoto's sees only recipes that satisfy both. For the 6 conditions with a healing-stage model, recipes also carry computed stage-specific cautions beside their hand-written notes.

My Kitchen and My Favorites are a different kind of reading: nothing in them is bundled, and they are computed from the person's data every time either lens opens. My Kitchen shelves every side, salad, soup and everything else saved from the builders, one shelf per builder, each entry carrying the same computed detail Recipes gets (an ingredient list, nutrition highlights, and cautions for whichever of the 19 conditions the person tracks) without the hand-written instructions only a curated recipe has. My Favorites has the same shape for favorited builds, plus a shelf for favorite meals, each built from its saved components at the portion favorited. Every entry has Schedule (a date, time and meal-type picker reaching up to two years out) and Share (a readable ingredient list plus a link through the phone's share sheet; someone with the app sees a preview before a copy is added to their My Kitchen, footnoted with who sent it).

A head-to-head diet comparison sits in the reading: a network meta-analysis of 121 trials and roughly 22,000 people testing 14 named diets directly against each other, plus a synthesis entry comparing composition, evidence tier and documented deficiency risk across every diet the Library covers.

No topic reads as one long shelf: the biggest Health Literacy topics and the larger condition topics (Hashimoto's Diet & Food, Prostate Health's Core Science, and more) are divided into named subgroups, each checked against the corpus so no entry is dropped or duplicated.

What it does

Built

A screen of its own, opened from the corner tile of the main menu and closed with its X, covering personal details, unit-system preference, navigation-hand setting, a multi-select for which of the 19 tracked conditions apply, and a visual-preferences panel: a choice of main-button icon (the Lifestead mark, any tracked condition's icon, or a set of animal and pollinator icons), an opt-out for the shared background photo and per-tab background images, and one of 12 flat, static colour combinations, which the header and footer accent lines and every selection ring take their colour from. Nothing on screen animates on its own, which keeps the battery from draining while the app sits open. A "Profile" title with its info button stays fixed at the top while the rest of the screen scrolls under it.

A Ground Theme picker (Navy, Teal, Purple, Charcoal, Burgundy, with Deep Teal the default) sets the base background colour across every screen and applies the moment one is picked. The Home Screen card holds the section visibility toggles and reorder controls described in the Home section, and the Header Growth card has an on/off switch for the header dots and growth-mark bands.

Two text rules apply across the whole app. No bold anywhere: all eight typography tiers are regular weight, so hierarchy is carried by size alone, and emphasis in the reading shows in the reading accent colour at regular weight. A drop shadow on all text: every text style carries one, sized to its text so small type stays sharp. The one exception is the main menu and lens menu labels, which keep a stronger shadow because a normal one does not show against the menu background.

The data underneath it all

Every tab above draws from the same bundled reference database, and this is what makes the food side of the app work at all.

16,140 whole foods shown, from 9 national sources plus a Derived set

USDA, Canada's CNF, the UK's CoFID, Germany's BLS, Australia's AFCD, France's Ciqual, and Japan's MEXT, bundled as a compact SQLite asset, not a live network call. Every food carries up to 35 nutrient values and a per-source citation trail for its D1–D6 scoring. Norway (Matvaretabellen) and Sweden (Livsmedelsverket) are the 8th and 9th sources, 2,121 and 2,606 foods, kept out of default browsing because their names are in Norwegian and Swedish.

The 6 Dimensions of Food Friendliness (6-DFF)

A Hashimoto's-specific scoring rubric across six dimensions (goitrogenic load, gluten, additives, processing, fat quality, and more), each sub-criterion individually cited and evidence-tiered, not a black-box score.

Reuse-first condition scoring

Rather than build a whole new scoring system per condition, most of the 19 tracked conditions reuse an existing 6-DFF sub-criterion under a condition-specific framing (the same Omega-3 data reads very differently for Rheumatoid Arthritis vs. Psoriasis vs. Prostate Health). A handful of conditions (Type 1 Diabetes, Chronic Kidney Disease, Cardiovascular Disease) needed one new, data-backed sub-criterion of their own.

A living Reference Database Audit tool

A separate, standalone tool used to hand-review and curate which foods in the reference database are actually shown, hide near-duplicates, fix miscategorizations, and bulk-apply thousands of curation decisions directly against the live database. Each exported batch of decisions is resolved against the database as it stands when applied, not as it stood when the review began, so review and apply can happen in separate sessions without drifting out of step.

Architecture: built vs. planned

Nothing in this app passes through a server this project runs. There is no company server holding anyone's health data. A person's phone and computer keep each other current, and so do two people who have paired, through a folder in the person's cloud drive that the app writes to encrypted.

What is built

Built
  • Delivery: JavaScript reaches phones over the air and native changes need a rebuild. Developer and Test versions stay matched until it is time to give the app to testers, and the Windows installers are built from the same code.
  • The tester version: opens only on an approved device, after an agreement and a signed approval. An ended approval keeps every record and only covers them.
  • The database only grows: the code that runs at startup adds tables, columns and indexes and never drops, renames or empties one, so testers keep their records across every update.
  • App Lock and the vault: on phone and desktop. The vault holds records only, never tools. Medicines, conditions and appointments in it are only hidden by screens, so safety warnings and scoring keep reading them.
  • The locked phone: every notification is answered or dismissed by tapping on the locked phone, and capture over the lock screen never opens the database. What was captured is sealed to the lock's public key and written at the next unlock.
  • Desktop and sync: the same code in an Electron window. A person's two devices stay in step through a folder on the person's cloud drive, one encrypted copy per device. Every arrival is merged record by record, one device has the session at a time, and a save sends only what changed.
  • Between two people: the same merge, on an allowlist. Nothing crosses unless a relationship names it, and today that is only the shopping list.
  • Free and paid: the free/paid board is the authority. Safety is never behind a paywall on any tier: the emergency card, med and supplement timing warnings, and allergy cautions.
  • Languages of the app: one app carries every language, and the English stays in the source. Mexican Spanish is complete and follows the phone's language by default. Every entry is Claude's translation, not yet checked by a native speaker.

Named and not built: bees and beekeeping, the Diary, working life and a resume, family health history with a family tree, Contacts, what grows on the freed tab screens, the external data program, and a life's Inside Story. The unified whole-foods database is a separate project not wired into the app.

What is planned

Planned
  • Household and caregiver tiers: partner, child and caregiver links pass changes the way a person's phone and computer do, merging rather than overwriting, with a log naming who did what. The seats, the billing and the read-only household viewer are the plan: a free viewer whose one write is checking off the shopping list, a paid household tier that shares the meal plan and shopping list but never the owner's health records, a Guardian role for a parent's own minor children, and a Caregiver role for someone looking after another adult.
  • Subscription billing: Free, Individual, Partner, Household and Caregiver are worked out in full and not yet wired to payment.
  • Ingredient rotation: permanent and rotating ingredients, so a smoothie or salad that varies week to week is planned as one.
  • Fermentation strains: a saved batch records which of 7 cited bacterial strains went into it, and more strains are planned.
  • The reading in other languages: the app's screens are in English and Spanish, the reading itself is in English, and translating it is planned.
